Reply to Reviewer nyqg.

Your strongest point is the first weakness: the headline precision of 1.00 and false-positive rate of 0.00 come from 6 true positives and 12 true negatives. We agree. In the revised version we will remove the headline performance sentence from the abstract and report per-detector counts with exact (Clopper-Pearson) intervals in the main table. The pooled row will be marked reference only, because the cases are heterogeneous and partly correlated: precision {{N:pool_prec}}, recall {{N:pool_rec}}, false-positive rate {{N:pool_fpr}}. We do not read those figures as performance, and the abstract will lead with the failure analysis.

On the claim that every type has a measured failure instance: it is too strong, and we will remove it. In its place the revised abstract will say: "Types 1 to 3 have measured instances in our detectors, Type 1 also has an external structural instance, and Type 4 is supported counterfactually; we do not claim the catalogue is complete." We will add a table that states, for each type, the internal instances, the external instances and how each is quantified. Type 1 has five internal instances and an external one: the pattern was reachable and unguarded in {{N:t4_narrow}} of applicable sampled sites, or {{N:t4_broad}} on the alternative reading. Type 2 rests on one detector plus an exploratory second (detector 5's flagship ratio against its gate). Type 3 rests on two early false negatives and a redundant gate. Types 2 to 4 have no external instance.

Your first question asks what evidence establishes Type 4 as an observed failure mode. None does. Type 4 is a process hazard, and we did not observe it as a failure of a shipped detector. We will say so. The evidence is counterfactual. We pre-registered a rule for choosing between two variants of detector 5. The alternative variant had higher recall at demonstration scale and was tempting, and the pre-registered criterion rejected it. One case with unknown ground truth was excluded from scoring in advance, and it is the only case on which the two variants diverge, so scoring it would have decided the comparison on data with no ground truth. A separate arm that fitted detector 3's recognition vocabulary to the corpus did not help. We also did not move detector 1's threshold after seeing the near-miss. This shows what pre-registration prevented, not an error that occurred, and we will not present it as more than that.

Your second question asks when an agreement threshold or negative control is clinically meaningful. The 0.60 threshold is a conventional agreement benchmark with no clinical derivation. We will add this as a prescription for building new detectors, to be fixed before seeing data: a negative control is informative when it is a pair of operationalisations that a domain expert accepts as interchangeable, and a threshold is meaningful only if it sits clear of the spread of the statistic across such pairs. For detector 1 the same pair of implementations gives kappa {{N:d1_pop}} on MIMIC-IV and {{N:d1_pop_eicu}} on eICU-CRD, so the spread across databases is large compared with the 0.002 by which the MIMIC-IV control misses the threshold. We will not apply the prescription retroactively to detector 1, because moving its threshold now would be the Type 4 error.

Two related corrections. The flag probability that the paper called a model prediction for detector 1 ({{N:d1_audit_pred}} against {{N:d1_audit_full}} observed) is a consistency check, because both come from the same draws, and we will name it that. And we retract the statement that the near-miss worsened with more data: the cohort-bootstrap flag probability is {{N:d1_cohort_demo}} at demonstration scale and {{N:d1_cohort_full}} at full scale.
